Understanding genetics

What you need to know to read the calculator’s genotypes and predict the colours of the ducklings, without being a geneticist.

Basic concepts

Gene and locus
A gene controls a trait, for example “self-coloured or not”. Its position on the chromosome is called the locus. The calculator follows ten genes, written M, Li, E, Bl, C, B, R, D, Bu and Cr.
Allele
A version of a gene. The E gene exists in two versions: E (extended black, self-coloured) and e+ (not extended). The “+” always marks the version found in the wild duck.
Two copies of each gene
Every duck has two copies of each gene, one from the father and one from the mother, and passes one of them at random to each duckling. The two copies are written separated by a slash: E/e+.
Homozygous, heterozygous
Two identical copies (E/E): homozygous, the duck always passes on the same one. Two different copies (E/e+): heterozygous, it passes on one or the other, half the time each.
Dominant, recessive
A dominant allele shows as soon as it is present once: E/e+ is self-coloured. A recessive allele only shows in two copies: it takes c/c to make a white duck.
Incomplete dominance
One copy gives an intermediate result, two copies a stronger effect. Example: one dose of blue gives a blue duck, two doses an almost white splash.
Carrier (“split”)
A duck that has one copy of a recessive allele without showing it, like a mallard-coloured C+/c. It passes it on to half of its ducklings. This is where “surprises” at hatching come from.
Sex-linked
The D (brown) and Bu (buff) genes are on the Z chromosome. The drake has two Z, the duck only one (plus an empty W). A duck therefore has only one copy, written d/–: she always shows what she carries, and cannot be a hidden carrier.
Masking
Some genes hide the effect of others: white hides everything, extended black hides the mallard pattern. Hidden genes are still passed on.

How the colour is built

The calculator reads the genotype in this order. At each step, the first question answered “yes” wins over the following ones.

  1. White (C)? With c/c, the duck is white with an orange bill. Everything else is hidden.
  2. Extended black (E)? With at least one E, the duck is self-coloured (black at base). The mallard pattern (M and Li) is hidden.
  3. Otherwise, mallard pattern. M sets the pattern: mallard, dusky (no eye stripes) or restricted (Appleyard). Li sets the ground colour: dark phase, trout (lighter) or silver (almost white).
  4. Dilutions. Bl lightens to blue (one or two doses), d turns black into brown, bu into buff. They add up (table below).
  5. White markings. R adds the runner pied pattern, b/b a bib.
  6. Crest. Cr adds a pompom on the head, regardless of colour.

The calculated colour name is descriptive: dilutions (blue, double-blue, brown, buff), then phase (trout, spotted, silver), then pattern (mallard, dusky). Variety names use the usual British or American name when there is one, and the French name otherwise (jaune pois). Names of blue + brown combinations on the mallard pattern vary between breeders and countries: the genotype is what counts.

The ten genes

Step-by-step examples

Two blue ducks

Each parent is Bl/bl+ and passes on Bl half the time.

Result: ¼ black (bl+/bl+), ½ blue, ¼ splash (Bl/Bl).

Remember: blue does not breed true. For 100 % blues, mate a black with a splash.

Chocolate drake × black duck

The father is d/d: he gives d to all his ducklings. The mother, D+/–, gives her Z (with D+) to her sons and her W (without a D gene) to her daughters.

Result: black sons carrying brown (D+/d), chocolate daughters (d/–).

Remember: with a sex-linked gene, the direction of the cross matters. The reverse cross (black drake × chocolate duck) gives only blacks, and the sons are carriers.

A black hiding its hand

A black E/e+ mated to a silver (e+/e+) passes on extended black half the time.

Result: half black, half not self-coloured. The colour of the non-self ducklings depends on the mallard pattern hidden under the parent’s black, which cannot be seen.

Remember: mating a duck with a dominant colour to a duck with recessive colours reveals what it hides.

Two mallards giving a white

Both parents are C+/c: mallard-coloured, carrying white.

Result: ¼ white, ¾ mallard, two thirds of which are carriers.

Two crested birds

Each parent is Cr/cr+.

Result: ¼ Cr/Cr (deformities, often not viable), ½ crested, ¼ without crest. Always mate a crested bird with a non-crested one.
